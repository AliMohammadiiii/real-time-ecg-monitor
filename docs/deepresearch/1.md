This quick scan points to a conservative thesis design: keep the AD8232/Arduino chain as an acquisition front end, do the serious DSP on the computer, use a modified Pan–Tompkins/Hamilton-style detector for R peaks, and treat P/T delineation plus arrhythmia warnings as SQI-gated, non-diagnostic outputs. The literature I found is strongest for QRS detection and weakest for reliable P-wave detection in noisy single-lead recordings, which is exactly the boundary your thesis should make explicit. [^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6]

**Executive summary and final recommendation**

Use 250 Hz acquisition, a simple serial packet from Arduino, and Python-side processing. For the first version, choose a modified Pan–Tompkins/Hamilton-style detector as the R-peak engine, add a lightweight SQI gate before any morphology analysis, and estimate P/Q/S/T only when the signal is good enough; if you want a more elegant second-stage delineator, phasor or wavelet methods are the best upgrade path from the papers I found. Pan–Tompkins is still the practical baseline, but the noisy-wearable literature consistently says its performance drops under ambulatory artifacts, while Hamilton-style and hybrid wavelet methods improve robustness. [^1][^2][^6]

Keep the arrhythmia module rule-based and explicitly educational. A prototype paper used BPM values below 60 and above 100 as warning bands, but that should stay labeled as a non-diagnostic flag, not a clinical rule. I would not make a lightweight ML classifier the main thesis claim unless you can assemble enough labeled data to validate it properly across datasets and your own recordings. [^7]

**1. Scope and Safety Boundary**

The AD8232 datasheet itself is explicit that the chip-level circuit is not a complete medical system: it is a signal-conditioning front end, not a validated diagnostic device, and the manufacturer says additional effort is needed to satisfy medical safety guidance. That fits your thesis framing well, because the system can be described as educational, exploratory, and non-diagnostic without overclaiming clinical validity. 

**2. ECG Signal and Low-Cost Single-Lead Constraints**

Single-lead ECG is workable for heart-rate and rough rhythm screening, but it is much less forgiving than multi-lead ECG for morphology. In a single-lead quality study, visual QRS detection reached 100% in healthy subjects, automatic QRS detection 87%, and P-wave visual detection 74%; in arrhythmia patients, automatic QRS was 80% and P-wave visual detection 95% in sinus rhythm, but the paper still shows that body position, electrode placement, baseline amplitude, and QRS amplitude matter a lot. The same study found that stable automatic QRS needed QRS amplitude above 0.60 mV and baseline amplitude below 0.20 mV in a sitting posture, which is a useful thesis-friendly way to explain why motion and electrode placement matter so much. 

That single-lead limitation is why P and T should be presented as “estimated fiducials” rather than guaranteed physiological truth. In the QT database literature, the annotations are not perfect, and a later reliability paper says that a significant percentage of QTDB annotations have been reported as inaccurate, especially for low-amplitude waves. [^5]

**3. Real-Time QRS/R-Peak Detection Algorithms**

Pan–Tompkins is still the best place to start if you want a detector that is simple, explainable, and fast enough for real time. A 2008 comparison of derivative-based detectors found that Hamilton–Tompkins achieved the highest detection accuracy in that test set, but it also had the largest timing error; the same paper says these derivative-based methods are computationally efficient and thus ideal for real-time analysis. A more recent hybrid paper also states that Pan–Tompkins remains the best choice for real-time QRS detection, but becomes less effective under noisy wearable conditions. [^1][^2]

Wavelet-based QRS detection is the strongest “upgrade” family I found for noisy single-lead data. The wavelet papers and hybrid wavelet studies repeatedly report high QRS sensitivity on PhysioNet-style databases and say the method is robust to baseline drift and artifact, although P-wave detection is still much harder than QRS detection. In other words, wavelets are useful when your thesis needs better noise tolerance than plain Pan–Tompkins, but they are better framed as a second-stage enhancement than as the first implementation choice. [^6]

Phasor-transform and multiscale delineators are also attractive because they are mathematically clean and computationally light. One phasor-transform paper explicitly describes the method as having low computational cost and mathematical simplicity, and multiscale fiducial estimators on the QT database report very high sensitivity with mean errors below 1 ms for onset/offset points. These are promising choices for P/T delineation after you already have reliable R peaks. 

I did not find a strong direct matched-filter paper in this scan, so I would treat matched filtering as optional and template-dependent rather than as the main thesis detector. That is not a claim that matched filters are bad; it is just the pattern of what surfaced in this search.

**5. ECG PQRST Delineation Methods**

For a bachelor’s thesis, the cleanest story is: detect R first, then delineate Q/S locally around the R peak, and treat P/T as conditional estimates. Time-window and local-extrema methods are easy to explain and implement, but they are the least reliable under noise and morphology variation. Derivative-based methods are still useful for QRS onset/offset, but they do not solve the low-amplitude P-wave problem by themselves. [^5]

Wavelet-based delineation is the strongest general-purpose option if you want P, Q, S, and T from one framework. ECGdeli reports median differences below 4 samples for P and QRS markers on QTDB, and a multiscale parameter-estimation paper reports high sensitivity with mean errors below 1 ms for onset/offset fiducials. At the same time, another wavelet study reports that P-wave detection can collapse to about 57% sensitivity/precision while QRS and T stay much stronger, which is the clearest reason to keep your P-wave claims modest. [^4]

| Method | Detects | Strengths | Weaknesses | Single-lead suitability | Complexity | Recommended use in this project |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time-window / local extrema | Approximate P, Q, S, T locations around R | Very simple, easy to explain | Fragile under noise and morphology changes | Moderate for QRS/Q/S, weak for P/T | Very low | Fallback or visualization only |
| Derivative-based | Slope changes, QRS onset/offset | Good for timing around R | P/T often unstable | High for QRS, moderate for T | Low | Baseline for first prototype |
| Wavelet-based | Onsets, offsets, peaks for P/QRS/T | Strong noise handling, good on QTDB | Needs tuning; P remains hardest | High if SQI is good | Moderate | Best thesis choice for P/T |
| Phasor / multiscale | Full fiducial set | Low computational cost, accurate on QTDB  | More math than time-window methods | High | Moderate | Good upgrade path |
| Deep learning | Fiducials via segmentation | Potentially very accurate on curated data | Data hungry, harder to explain, scope creep | Moderate in principle | High | Leave out of main build |

**6. Signal Quality Index for Single-Lead ECG**

The most practical SQI set for your system is a small fusion of qSQI, pSQI, kSQI, and basSQI, plus hard gates for lead-off/flatline/saturation and RR plausibility. One Frontiers paper explicitly uses that exact heuristic SQI set, and a 2024 benchmark found that SQIs are inconsistent with one another and often disagree with cardiologist judgment, which means you should use them as confidence gates rather than as absolute truth. A PQRST-consistency SQI is also useful for suppressing false ischemia or morphology warnings during motion artifact. [^4]

A simple thesis-friendly confidence score can be: start at 100; subtract heavily for lead-off, flatline, or saturation; subtract for high basSQI, low qSQI, or implausible RR intervals; and only allow P/T delineation when the score is above a predefined threshold. If you want a concrete amplitude gate from the single-lead quality study, the paper’s stable-QRS region was QRS amplitude >0.60 mV and baseline amplitude <0.20 mV in the sitting posture.

**7. Non-Diagnostic Arrhythmia Warning Rules**

For a safe undergraduate system, keep the warning logic intentionally narrow: bradycardia flag, tachycardia flag, irregular-RR flag, premature-beat suspicion, wide-QRS suspicion, and poor-signal/unreliable-analysis warning. A prototype Arduino ECG paper used BPM below 60 and above 100 as warning bands, which is fine for an educational flag, but it should be written as a heuristic, not a diagnosis. [^7]

I would make the poor-signal warning the most important one, because it prevents the system from overinterpreting noise. The single-lead quality literature gives you a defensible way to do that: if QRS amplitude is low, baseline wander is high, qSQI is poor, or the lead-off state is active, suppress all arrhythmia warnings and show “signal unreliable.” For premature beats and wide QRS, I would keep the rules exploratory unless you can validate them against annotated records; I did not retrieve a strong threshold source for those in this scan, so they should not be the thesis’s central claims.

**8. Evaluation Datasets**

MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database is the right primary dataset for R-peak detection and arrhythmia-beat benchmarking. The official PhysioNet page says it contains 48 half-hour excerpts of two-channel ambulatory ECG recordings, digitized at 360 samples per second per channel with 11-bit resolution over a 10 mV range. That makes it ideal for beat detection, RR analysis, and basic arrhythmia labels, but not for perfect P/T delineation. [^8]

QT Database is the right dataset for fiducial-point evaluation. The official documentation says it contains 105 fifteen-minute two-channel excerpts, sampled at 250 Hz, with P/QRS/T/U annotations for manually selected beats. It is the dataset to use for P/Q/S/T timing error, but it is not a perfect stand-in for noisy real-world acquisition: the records were selected to avoid significant baseline wander or other artifacts, only normal beats were annotated, and later work warns that some annotations are inaccurate. [^9][^5]

MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database is the right dataset for noise robustness. The official page says it includes 12 half-hour ECG recordings and 3 half-hour noise recordings, with baseline wander, muscle artifact, and electrode motion artifact separated into the noise tracks. Its weakness is diversity: later work notes that it is based on only two clean ECG recordings, so it is useful as a stress test but not as the only noise benchmark. [^3]

**9. Evaluation Metrics and Protocol**

For R-peak detection, report sensitivity, positive predictive value, F1-score, and timing error in milliseconds. Sensitivity and PPV should be computed beat-by-beat with a clear matching tolerance, and timing error should be reported as absolute error relative to the reference annotation. For P/Q/S/T, use mean or median absolute fiducial error and coverage rate; for real-time performance, measure end-to-end latency, update rate, and dropped-packet rate; for warning logic, report false-alarm rate and warning precision. Those metrics are standard enough that they do not need special justification, but they do need to be separated by task so the reader can see what the system actually does well. 

| Metric | Formula or definition | Used for | Interpretation | Required annotation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sensitivity | TP/(TP+FN) | R-peak detection | Higher is better | Beat annotations | Report on clean and noisy subsets separately |
| PPV | TP/(TP+FP) | R-peak detection | Higher is better | Beat annotations | Especially important for false alarms |
| F1-score | 2·(Sensitivity·PPV)/(Sensitivity+PPV) | R-peak detection | Single-number balance | Beat annotations | Compute yourself even if papers do not report it |
| Timing error | Mean or median absolute ms difference | R-peak and fiducials | Lower is better | R peaks / fiducial labels | Use ms, not samples, for readability |
| Fiducial error | Absolute error for P/Q/S/T onsets, peaks, offsets | Delineation | Lower is better | QTDB-style annotations | Only trust high-SQI segments |
| Latency | Acquisition-to-display or acquisition-to-decision delay | Real-time behavior | Lower is better | None or timestamp logs | Report mean and worst-case |
| False-alarm rate | Warnings per hour or per minute | Warning module | Lower is better | Human-labeled events or proxies | Separate true from nuisance alarms |
| SQI operating characteristics | Accuracy / AUROC / sensitivity / specificity vs expert quality labels | Signal quality gate | Higher is better | Expert quality labels | Use on synthetic-noise and real-recorded data |

**10. Arduino/AD8232 Implementation Considerations**

The AD8232 should be treated as the analog front end: it conditions the ECG and gives you a usable single-lead signal, but the filter chain and safety context still need to be handled carefully. The datasheet shows a 0.5 Hz high-pass and 40 Hz low-pass configuration for ECG waveform monitoring, and it explicitly warns that the shown circuit is not a complete medical system design. 

For the Arduino side, the practical prototype literature is consistent: Arduino is used mainly as a sampler and ADC, not as the main DSP engine. One paper describes the AD8232 sensor interfacing with Arduino Nano and another reports digitization through the 10-bit ADC of the ATmega328, which is enough for an educational build but reinforces why the detailed processing should move to Python on the computer. [^10]

My recommended acquisition format is: 250 Hz sampling, a packet with sequence number, timestamp, raw ADC value, and lead-off state, then USB serial to Python. Use Python libraries such as `pyserial`, `numpy`, `scipy`, `matplotlib` or `pyqtgraph`, `pandas`, and `wfdb`; use a causal filter online for live display and a zero-phase filter only for offline evaluation on datasets. The 250 Hz choice is defensible because it matches QTDB, while 360 Hz is also acceptable if serial throughput and plotting remain stable. [^9][^8]

**11. Rule-Based vs Lightweight ML Decision**

Keep the arrhythmia module rule-based for the main thesis. That gives you transparency, easy debugging, and a clear safety boundary; it also prevents the project from drifting into an unvalidated diagnostic classifier. If you add ML at all, I would confine it to signal-quality estimation or a small appendix experiment, and I would prefer logistic regression or a linear SVM over random forests because the smaller models are easier to explain and easier to defend with limited labeled data. The best practical use of ML here is not diagnosis, but possibly SQI refinement on top of the rule-based system. 

**12. Final Recommended System Architecture**

- Arduino/AD8232 acquisition at 250 Hz, 10-bit ADC, serial packet with timestamp, sequence number, raw sample, and lead-off flag. [^10]
- Computer-side Python pipeline: bandpass around the AD8232 pass band, optional 50/60 Hz notch, then modified Pan–Tompkins/Hamilton-style R detection. [^1][^2]
- SQI gate before all morphology analysis: qSQI, pSQI, kSQI, basSQI, lead-off, flatline, and RR plausibility. [^4]
- P/Q/S/T delineation only on high-quality beats, preferably with wavelet or phasor/multiscale methods. 
- Feature extraction: HR, RR mean/SD, RR irregularity, QRS width, P/T amplitude, beat-to-beat morphology consistency.
- Warning logic: educational brady/tachy, irregular-RR suspicion, premature-beat suspicion, wide-QRS suspicion, and poor-signal suppression.
- Evaluation pipeline: MIT-BIH Arrhythmia for R/QRS, QTDB for fiducials, NSTDB for noise robustness, then a small real AD8232 recording set for face validity. [^9][^8][^3][^5]

**13. Thesis Integration Notes**

In the literature review, group the discussion as acquisition front end, QRS detection, delineation, SQI, and dataset validation; that structure will read more cleanly than a paper-by-paper list. In the methods chapter, make the safety boundary explicit: acquisition is hardware, detection is signal processing, and warnings are only preliminary educational flags. In the evaluation chapter, separate dataset benchmarking from real-device testing, because the datasets are cleaner and more controlled than live AD8232 recordings. [^9][^8][^3][^5]
