## Executive summary and final recommendation

For this undergraduate ECG system, the safest and most defensible design is **not** a diagnostic arrhythmia classifier. It should be framed as an **educational real-time ECG acquisition and signal-processing platform** that estimates R-peaks, approximate P/Q/S/T fiducial points, simple beat-to-beat features, signal quality, and **preliminary non-diagnostic warnings**.

**Final recommendation:** use a **modified Pan–Tompkins / Hamilton-style QRS detector** as the primary real-time R-peak detector, implemented on the Python computer side rather than on the Arduino. Use the Arduino only for deterministic sampling, lead-off status reading, sequence numbering, and USB/Serial transfer. Use **250 Hz or 360 Hz sampling**: 250 Hz is adequate for undergraduate PQRST timing experiments and aligns with QTDB-style fiducial evaluation, while 360 Hz aligns directly with MIT-BIH Arrhythmia and Noise Stress Test records [12], [13], [14]. MIT-BIH Arrhythmia contains 48 half-hour two-channel ECG records sampled at 360 Hz with cardiologist beat annotations, making it the most practical R-peak benchmark for this project [12]. ([physionet.org][1]) QTDB contains 105 records and includes expert waveform boundary annotations for selected beats, making it the most relevant public dataset for P/QRS/T delineation evaluation [13]. ([physionet.org][2])

Use **simple confidence-aware PQRST delineation**, not full clinical ECG measurement. Q and S can usually be estimated from local extrema around a reliable R-peak. P and T waves are less reliable in noisy, single-lead AD8232 recordings because they are lower-amplitude, morphology-dependent, and more affected by baseline wander, motion artifact, electrode placement, and heart-rate overlap. Wavelet delineation is academically strong but heavier; a windowed local-extrema/derivative method is more appropriate for a Bachelor’s implementation, with QTDB used to quantify its error [4], [5], [13].

For “arrhythmia risk,” keep the module **rule-based**. It should output warnings such as “possible bradycardia,” “possible tachycardia,” “irregular RR pattern,” “premature-beat suspicion,” “wide-QRS suspicion,” and “poor signal/unreliable analysis.” These must be explicitly labeled **educational and non-diagnostic**. Adult bradycardia and tachycardia thresholds are commonly framed around resting heart rate below 60 bpm and above 100 bpm, but interpretation is context-dependent and requires clinical context [21], [22]. ([NCBI][3]) ([NCBI][4]) A QRS duration of 120 ms is a common boundary for “wide QRS,” but estimating QRS width from a noisy single-lead educational device should be treated as low-confidence unless signal quality is high [23]. ([NCBI][5])

# 1. Scope and Safety Boundary

The proposed system is an **educational biomedical signal-processing prototype**, not a medical device. It should not claim to diagnose arrhythmia, ischemia, conduction disease, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, QT prolongation, or any other clinical condition. Its outputs should be described as **signal-processing estimates** and **preliminary warnings**.

The AD8232 is a single-lead ECG/biopotential analog front end designed to amplify and filter small biopotential signals before acquisition by an ADC or microcontroller; it includes an instrumentation amplifier, filtering support, right-leg-drive circuitry, and lead-off detection, but it is not a complete certified diagnostic ECG system [19]. ([Analog Devices][6]) The Arduino Uno Rev3 is a general-purpose microcontroller board based on the ATmega328P with six analog inputs, 5 V logic, 16 MHz clock, USB connection, and limited memory; it is not a medical-isolated acquisition platform [20]. ([Arduino Official Store][7])

**Safety boundary for thesis text:**

Human testing should be limited to consenting healthy adult volunteers under supervision. The acquisition system should preferably be battery-powered or connected through appropriate isolation. The subject should not be connected to any unsafe mains-referenced equipment while electrodes are attached. Do not use the system for symptomatic people, emergency situations, pacemaker users, pregnant subjects, children, or clinical decision-making. Any warning message should include wording such as:

> “Educational ECG signal-processing warning only. This is not a medical diagnosis. If symptoms are present, seek qualified medical care.”

# 2. ECG Signal and Low-Cost Single-Lead Constraints

A standard ECG contains P wave, QRS complex, and T wave components. For a single-lead AD8232/Arduino system, the most robust target is usually **R-peak detection**, because the QRS complex has the largest slope and amplitude relative to P and T waves. P and T detection are inherently less reliable because their amplitude, polarity, and visibility depend strongly on lead placement, morphology, heart rate, and noise.

The AD8232 is intended to extract, amplify, and filter small biopotential signals in noisy conditions such as motion or remote electrode placement, but its performance still depends on electrode quality, skin contact, cable motion, analog filter design, power supply noise, and common-mode interference [19]. ([Analog Devices][6]) Its datasheet describes integrated high-pass filtering to reduce motion and electrode half-cell potential, optional low-pass filtering using the uncommitted op amp, right-leg drive for common-mode rejection, and lead-off detection [19]. ([Analog Devices][6])

The Arduino should not be responsible for heavy ECG interpretation. Its role should be:

1. sample the analog ECG output at a fixed sampling rate;

2. read AD8232 lead-off pins;

3. add a sequence number and timestamp;

4. transmit raw samples to the computer;

5. avoid filtering or classification except perhaps simple range checks.

A PC/laptop running Python is far more suitable for real-time plotting, filtering, adaptive QRS detection, SQI computation, feature extraction, logging, and evaluation.

# 3. Real-Time QRS/R-Peak Detection Algorithms

## 3.1 Pan–Tompkins

The Pan–Tompkins algorithm remains the best starting point for this project because it is causal, interpretable, lightweight, and historically designed for real-time QRS detection [1]. Its classical stages are:

1. band-pass filtering to emphasize QRS energy;

2. derivative filtering to emphasize slope;

3. squaring to make all slopes positive and amplify large slopes;

4. moving-window integration to estimate QRS energy envelope;

5. adaptive thresholding, refractory logic, and search-back rules.

Its main limitation is that large artifacts, baseline wander, motion noise, tall T waves, and abnormal morphology can cause false positives or missed beats. Recent stress-testing work argues that QRS detectors should be evaluated not only by F-score but also by temporal jitter because a detector can detect the right beat count while still producing poor timing precision [3]. Scite-indexed evidence also identifies noisy/low-quality ECG as a known weakness of the original Pan–Tompkins method and motivates modified variants.

## 3.2 Modified Pan–Tompkins

A modified Pan–Tompkins implementation is recommended rather than a literal 1985 implementation. Useful modifications include:

* explicit baseline-removal before QRS detection;

* a notch filter only when power-line interference is detected;

* adaptive signal/noise thresholds;

* R-peak relocation on the less-distorted filtered ECG after envelope detection;

* polarity adaptation for different electrode placements;

* rejection of peaks inside a physiological refractory interval;

* SQI gating before accepting beats.

This keeps the algorithm explainable while improving robustness for AD8232/Arduino recordings.

## 3.3 Hamilton/Tompkins-style detector

Hamilton/Tompkins-style detectors are also appropriate because they use real-time filtering, slope/energy information, adaptive thresholds, and practical decision rules [2]. They are often simpler to implement than wavelet delineators and are useful as a second detector for SQI agreement: if Pan–Tompkins and Hamilton-style detectors disagree strongly, the segment can be marked low-confidence.

## 3.4 Wavelet-based QRS detection

Wavelet-based QRS detection can achieve strong performance because QRS complexes are localized transient events with characteristic frequency content. Wavelet methods are also central to ECG delineation literature [4], [5]. However, they require more parameter choices, more careful implementation, and more explanation. They are suitable as an offline comparison or advanced extension, but not necessary as the primary undergraduate real-time detector.

## 3.5 Matched filter and template methods

Matched filters compare the incoming ECG with a QRS template. They can work well when morphology is stable, but they degrade when morphology changes, electrode placement changes, or noise distorts the QRS. For this project, template correlation is better used as an **SQI feature** or morphology-consistency check than as the only detector.

## 3.6 Lightweight ML detectors

A lightweight ML detector is possible, but it is not recommended as the primary detector. It requires labeled training data, cross-subject validation, careful handling of class imbalance, and clear claims about generalization. For a Bachelor’s thesis, classical detectors are more explainable and easier to evaluate rigorously.

# 5. ECG PQRST Delineation Methods

ECG delineation estimates the timing of peaks, onsets, and offsets of P waves, QRS complexes, and T waves. In this project, delineation should be described as **estimation**, not clinical measurement.

The most academically established approach for full ECG delineation is wavelet-based delineation. Martínez et al. proposed and evaluated a wavelet-based ECG delineator on standard databases, including P, QRS, and T wave boundaries [4]. Wavelet methods are attractive because they localize sharp QRS components and smoother P/T components at different scales [4], [5]. However, they are more complex than necessary for the first implementation.

A simpler undergraduate method is:

* detect R-peaks first;

* estimate Q and S using local extrema or slope changes around R;

* estimate P in a pre-R window;

* estimate T in a post-R window;

* attach confidence labels based on SQI, peak prominence, RR interval, and morphology consistency.

Suggested windows at 250–360 Hz:

* Q search: approximately 80 ms before R to 10 ms before R;

* S search: approximately 10 ms after R to 100 ms after R;

* P search: approximately 220 ms before R to 80 ms before R;

* T search: approximately 120 ms after R to 400 ms after R, adjusted for heart rate.

These windows are design heuristics and must be validated against QTDB rather than presented as universal physiology. QTDB provides expert annotations for P, QRS, T, and U waveform markers on selected beats, which makes it the correct dataset for measuring fiducial timing error [13]. ([physionet.org][2])

# 6. Signal Quality Index for Single-Lead ECG

A Signal Quality Index is essential because a low-cost single-lead system will frequently encounter loose electrodes, motion artifact, baseline wander, power-line interference, saturation, and unstable morphology. SQI should not be an optional feature; it should gate the detector and warning module.

Relevant SQI ideas include detector agreement, QRS power concentration, kurtosis, baseline relative power, morphology consistency, and RR plausibility. Scite-indexed literature describes single-lead SQI approaches using features such as R-peak detector agreement, QRS spectral power distribution, kurtosis, and baseline relative power.  Other cited SQI literature emphasizes that portable/wearable ECG quality assessment often uses RR consistency, QRS coherence, and PQRST morphology consistency, but fiducial-based SQI can fail when the ECG is very noisy or morphology varies strongly.

For this project, use a **two-layer SQI**:

**Hard rejection layer:**

* AD8232 lead-off detected;

* ADC saturation near 0 or full scale;

* flatline or near-zero variance;

* impossible sample timing or serial packet loss;

* extremely high noise energy.

**Soft confidence layer:**

* baseline wander ratio;

* power-line interference ratio;

* high-frequency noise ratio;

* detector agreement;

* template correlation;

* RR plausibility;

* P/T visibility confidence.

# 7. Non-Diagnostic Arrhythmia Warning Rules

The warning module should be placed **after** SQI gating. If signal quality is poor, the only output should be “analysis unreliable,” not arrhythmia warnings.

Recommended educational warnings:

## 7.1 Bradycardia warning

Trigger when median heart rate over a stable window, such as 10–30 s, is below 60 bpm and SQI is good. Adult sinus bradycardia is commonly defined as sinus rhythm below 60 bpm, although clinical interpretation depends on symptoms, sleep, fitness, medication, and patient context [21]. ([NCBI][3])

Suggested message:

> “Possible low heart rate pattern detected. Educational warning only; not a diagnosis.”

## 7.2 Tachycardia warning

Trigger when median heart rate over a stable window is above 100 bpm at rest and SQI is good. Sinus tachycardia is commonly associated with heart rate above 100 bpm, but exercise, stress, fever, dehydration, and many other non-arrhythmic causes can raise heart rate [22]. ([NCBI][4])

## 7.3 Irregular RR warning

Compute RR intervals after artifact rejection. Use measures such as:

[

CV_{RR}=\frac{\sigma(RR)}{\mu(RR)}

]

and median absolute successive difference. A simple educational warning can be triggered when irregularity remains high over a window and SQI is good. Do **not** label this as atrial fibrillation; single-lead RR irregularity alone is insufficient for a diagnostic claim.

## 7.4 Premature-beat suspicion

A simple rule can flag:

[

RR_i < 0.8 \times median(RR)

]

followed by:

[

RR_{i+1} > 1.2 \times median(RR)

]

This can indicate a premature-beat-like pattern, but the thresholds are heuristic and must be presented as a signal-processing suspicion, not diagnosis.

## 7.5 Wide QRS warning

Trigger only if estimated QRS duration is at least 120 ms and QRS boundary confidence is high. A QRS duration of 120 ms is commonly used as the boundary for wide QRS complexes [23]. ([NCBI][5]) However, QRS width estimation from a noisy single lead is fragile, so the warning should say “possible wide-QRS morphology” rather than “bundle branch block” or “ventricular rhythm.”

## 7.6 Poor signal warning

If lead-off, saturation, flatline, heavy baseline wander, severe noise, or detector disagreement is present, suppress all rhythm warnings and show:

> “Poor ECG signal quality. Analysis is unreliable. Check electrodes and reduce movement.”

# 8. Evaluation Datasets

The evaluation should combine **dataset-based testing** and **real AD8232/Arduino testing**.

MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database is the main beat/R-peak benchmark. It includes 48 half-hour two-channel ambulatory ECG excerpts from 47 subjects, sampled at 360 Hz, with cardiologist annotations [12]. ([physionet.org][1]) The header for record 100 confirms two channels at 360 Hz with MLII and V5 lead names [12]. ([physionet.org][8])

QTDB is the primary delineation benchmark because it contains two-lead ECG records with manually annotated waveform boundaries for selected beats, including P, QRS, T, and U wave markers [13]. ([physionet.org][2])

MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database is the primary noise-robustness benchmark. It contains clean ECG records and calibrated noise recordings: baseline wander, muscle artifact, and electrode motion. Noise was added to MIT-BIH records 118 and 119 at SNR levels from 24 dB down to −6 dB [14]. ([physionet.org][9])

MIT-BIH P-Wave Annotations can be used for additional P-wave evaluation, but the official documentation notes that the database provides expert annotations for twelve MIT-BIH signals and does not guarantee every P wave is labeled correctly, so it should be used cautiously [15]. ([physionet.org][10])

INCART is useful for broader arrhythmia and morphology stress testing because it contains 75 annotated 12-lead half-hour records sampled at 257 Hz, but it is not a single-lead AD8232 dataset and includes patients with coronary artery disease and rhythm abnormalities [16]. ([physionet.org][11])

European ST-T Database is useful for ST/T morphology and long-duration signal behavior, with 90 two-hour two-signal records sampled at 250 Hz and annotated for ST/T episodes, rhythm, and signal quality [17]. ([physionet.org][12])

# 10. Evaluation Metrics and Protocol

## 10.1 R-peak detection

A detected R-peak is a true positive if it matches a reference beat annotation within a tolerance window. A common tolerance for beat detection is ±150 ms, but timing error should also be reported in milliseconds because high F1-score can hide systematic jitter [3].

**Table 5 — Evaluation metrics**

| Metric                    | Formula or definition                                               | Used for              | Interpretation                             | Required annotation                 | Notes                                   |

| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |

| Sensitivity / Recall      | (Se = TP/(TP+FN))                                                   | R-peak detection      | Fraction of true beats detected            | Beat annotations                    | High Se means few missed beats          |

| Positive Predictive Value | (PPV = TP/(TP+FP))                                                  | R-peak detection      | Fraction of detections that are true beats | Beat annotations                    | High PPV means few false alarms         |

| F1-score                  | (F1 = 2TP/(2TP+FP+FN))                                              | R-peak detection      | Balance of Se and PPV                      | Beat annotations                    | Useful summary, but hides timing jitter |

| Detection error rate      | ((FP+FN)/N_{beats})                                                 | R-peak detection      | Overall beat detection error               | Beat annotations                    | Common in QRS evaluation                |

| Timing error              | (t_{detected}-t_{reference}) in ms                                  | R-peak and fiducials  | Bias, MAE, SD, jitter                      | Fiducial annotations                | Report mean, median, MAE, SD            |

| P/Q/S/T fiducial error    | Difference between estimated and annotated fiducial time            | Delineation           | Accuracy of waveform markers               | QTDB/P-wave annotations             | Report separately for P, QRS, T         |

| Processing latency        | Output time minus sample acquisition time                           | Real-time performance | Delay experienced by user                  | System timestamps                   | Include filter and decision delay       |

| Update rate               | Plot/classification refresh frequency                               | Real-time UI          | Smoothness of visualization                | Runtime logs                        | Target stable real-time display         |

| SQI accuracy              | Correct poor/good classification or correlation with injected noise | Signal quality        | Whether SQI tracks degradation             | Noise labels or injected artifacts  | NSTDB is useful                         |

| Warning precision         | True warnings / all warnings                                        | Arrhythmia warning    | False-alarm tendency                       | Beat/rhythm annotations             | Only educational, not diagnosis         |

| False alarm rate          | False warnings per minute/hour                                      | Warning module        | Practical usability                        | Annotated or manually reviewed data | Important for real-time use             |

## 10.2 Suggested protocol

1. **Offline detector validation on MIT-BIH Arrhythmia:**

   Run the detector on all records. Report Se, PPV, F1, error rate, and timing error.

2. **Noise stress testing on NSTDB:**

   Test at SNR levels 24, 18, 12, 6, 0, and −6 dB. Report how Se, PPV, F1, timing error, and SQI degrade.

3. **Delineation validation on QTDB:**

   Use detected or reference R-peaks, estimate P/Q/S/T, and compare timing against available annotations.

4. **P-wave supplementary testing:**

   Use MIT-BIH P-wave annotations cautiously and report limitations.

5. **Real AD8232/Arduino testing:**

   Record controlled sessions: resting, mild movement, intentional loose electrode, power-line exposure, and quiet baseline. Measure latency, packet loss, SQI behavior, and visual stability.

6. **Warning evaluation:**

   Test rules on annotated records where possible. Report false alarms and explicitly state that the module is not clinically validated.

# 11. Arduino/AD8232 Implementation Considerations

## 11.1 AD8232 role

The AD8232 performs analog signal conditioning, not digital interpretation. It amplifies and filters the ECG before the Arduino ADC. The datasheet describes a high signal gain configuration, high-pass filtering for motion/electrode offset, optional low-pass filtering, right-leg drive, and lead-off detection [19]. ([Analog Devices][6])

## 11.2 Arduino role

The Arduino Uno Rev3 has limited computational resources: ATmega328P, 16 MHz clock, 2 KB SRAM, and six analog inputs [20]. ([Arduino Official Store][7]) It is sufficient for acquisition but not ideal for robust visualization, adaptive filtering, dataset evaluation, or real-time PQRST analysis. Therefore, use the Arduino as a data-acquisition bridge.

## 11.3 Recommended sampling rate

Use **250 Hz** if the priority is reliable serial streaming and compatibility with QTDB-like delineation experiments. Use **360 Hz** if the priority is direct comparison with MIT-BIH Arrhythmia and Noise Stress Test records. Avoid claiming that higher sampling automatically improves accuracy; poor electrodes, motion, and filtering usually dominate error in this project.

## 11.4 Serial packet format

Recommended simple CSV packet:

```text

S,<seq>,<micros>,<adc>,<lo_plus>,<lo_minus>

```

Example:

```text

S,18452,73808000,512,0,0

```

Where:

* `seq` is a monotonically increasing sample counter;

* `micros` is the Arduino timestamp;

* `adc` is the raw ADC value;

* `lo_plus` and `lo_minus` are AD8232 lead-off digital states.

For a more robust version, use a binary packet with start byte, sequence number, timestamp, ADC value, lead-off bits, and checksum. For an undergraduate thesis, CSV is easier to debug and explain; sequence numbers are essential for detecting dropped samples.

# 12. Rule-Based vs Lightweight ML Decision

The arrhythmia module should remain **rule-based** in the main thesis implementation.

A lightweight ML model such as logistic regression, SVM, decision tree, or random forest can be added only as an **offline exploratory extension**, not as the main warning engine. ML would require labeled data, subject-wise train/test separation, feature normalization, class-imbalance handling, validation on external datasets, and careful language to avoid medical diagnosis claims. A small classifier trained on public datasets may not generalize to noisy AD8232/Arduino recordings.

**Tradeoff analysis:**

| Option              | Advantages                                                        | Disadvantages                                          | Recommendation                  |

| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |

| Rule-based warnings | Transparent, easy to cite, easy to test, low risk of overclaiming | Limited accuracy; cannot diagnose specific arrhythmias | **Use in final system**         |

| Logistic regression | Explainable, low complexity                                       | Needs reliable labels and features                     | Optional offline experiment     |

| SVM                 | Good for small feature sets                                       | Harder to interpret; tuning burden                     | Not needed                      |

| Decision tree       | Explainable                                                       | Can overfit small datasets                             | Optional teaching demo          |

| Random forest       | Better robustness than one tree                                   | Less transparent; more evaluation burden               | Not recommended for main thesis |

| Deep learning       | Powerful with large data                                          | Overkill, high overclaiming risk, data-hungry          | Exclude                         |

**Best practical decision:** implement a rule-based module with conservative thresholds, SQI gating, and cautious wording. This fits the undergraduate scope and is defensible academically.

# 13. Final Recommended System Architecture

## 13.1 Acquisition

```text

Electrodes

  ↓

AD8232 analog front end

  ↓

Arduino ADC + lead-off pins

  ↓

USB/Serial packets with sequence number and timestamp

  ↓

Python receiver

```

Recommended sampling:

* 250 Hz for main prototype;

* 360 Hz if aligning with MIT-BIH evaluation;

* log actual timestamps to measure jitter.

## 13.2 Python libraries

Recommended:

* `numpy`, `scipy` for filtering and signal processing;

* `matplotlib` or `pyqtgraph` for real-time visualization;

* `wfdb` for PhysioNet datasets;

* `pandas` for logs and metrics;

* optional `neurokit2` for comparison experiments, not as a black-box replacement.

WFDB-Python supports reading WFDB records and streaming from PhysioNet using `pn_dir`, which is useful for reproducible evaluation [18]. ([wfdb.readthedocs.io][13])

## 13.3 Filtering pipeline

Use two parallel filtered signals:

**Visualization/delineation signal:**

* remove DC offset;

* high-pass around 0.5 Hz;

* low-pass around 35–40 Hz;

* optional 50 Hz notch if needed;

* preserve P and T waves as much as possible.

**QRS detection signal:**

* band-pass emphasizing QRS energy, for example around 5–15 Hz or 5–20 Hz;

* derivative;

* squaring;

* moving-window integration;

* adaptive thresholding.

Avoid zero-phase filters in the real-time path because they are non-causal. Zero-phase filtering may be used only for offline plots or evaluation.

## 13.4 QRS detection

Use a modified Pan–Tompkins detector with:

* adaptive signal/noise thresholds;

* 200 ms refractory period;

* search-back for missed beats;

* R-peak relocation on filtered ECG;

* detector confidence;

* optional Hamilton-style detector agreement.

## 13.5 PQRST delineation

For each accepted R-peak:

1. estimate Q and S from local extrema and slope changes around R;

2. estimate QRS duration only when boundaries are clear;

3. estimate P in a pre-R window;

4. estimate T in a post-R window;

5. attach confidence to every marker;

6. suppress P/T markers when SQI or peak prominence is poor.

## 13.6 Feature extraction

Extract:

* RR interval;

* instantaneous heart rate;

* median heart rate;

* RR variability measures;

* QRS duration estimate;

* R amplitude;

* beat template correlation;

* P/T visibility flags;

* SQI score.

## 13.7 SQI module

Use hard rejection plus soft confidence:

```text

if lead_off or saturation or flatline:

    confidence = 0

elif severe_packet_loss:

    confidence = 0

else:

    confidence = weighted_score(

        detector_agreement,

        template_correlation,

        rr_plausibility,

        baseline_wander_score,

        powerline_score,

        high_frequency_noise_score

    )

```

## 13.8 Warning module

Only run warnings when SQI is acceptable:

* HR < 60 bpm → possible bradycardia warning;

* HR > 100 bpm → possible tachycardia warning;

* high RR irregularity → irregular rhythm warning;

* short RR followed by long RR → premature-beat suspicion;

* QRS ≥ 120 ms with high confidence → possible wide-QRS warning;

* poor SQI → unreliable analysis warning.

## 13.9 Evaluation pipeline

```text

PhysioNet MIT-BIH → R-peak metrics

PhysioNet NSTDB → noise robustness and SQI

PhysioNet QTDB → P/QRS/T timing error

Real AD8232 recordings → latency, packet loss, SQI, usability

```

# 14. Thesis Integration Notes

For the **literature review**, emphasize:

* classical real-time QRS detection;

* Pan–Tompkins as the baseline;

* modified classical detectors for noisy single-lead ECG;

* wavelet methods as strong but more complex delineation methods;

* SQI as necessary for low-cost acquisition.

* Arduino as acquisition only;

* Python as signal-processing engine;

* modified Pan–Tompkins detector;

* confidence-aware PQRST estimation;

* SQI-gated warning rules.

For the **implementation chapter**, include:

* circuit and electrode placement;

* AD8232 output and lead-off pins;

* Arduino packet format;

* sampling loop;

* Python receiver;

* real-time filtering;

* visualization;

* logging.

For the **evaluation chapter**, separate:

1. public dataset evaluation;

2. noise stress testing;

3. real hardware testing;

4. warning-rule behavior;

5. limitations.

Use cautious language throughout:

* “R-peak detection” rather than “arrhythmia diagnosis”;

* “PQRST estimation” rather than “clinical interval measurement”;

* “warning” rather than “diagnosis”;

* “poor signal / unreliable analysis” whenever SQI is low.

# 15. Weak or Excluded Sources

Excluded source types:

* random Arduino ECG tutorials;

* commercial hobbyist blogs;

* unsourced GitHub repositories;

* marketing pages for ECG modules;

* papers without traceable metadata;

* projects claiming arrhythmia diagnosis from AD8232 without proper validation;

* Wikipedia-style summaries.

Weak but potentially useful context:

* AD8232/Arduino prototype papers can be cited only for implementation examples, not for clinical performance.

* Pan–Tompkins++ and similar recent preprints may be discussed as modified-detector examples, but they should not replace established peer-reviewed sources unless their validation and metadata are verified [24].

* Deep-learning ECG classifiers are out of scope unless used only as a literature contrast.
